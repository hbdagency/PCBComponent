Why it matters

A footprint error costs more than a footprint.

It shows up as a rotated connector, a tombstoned 0402, or a re-spin two weeks before launch. We treat component data as engineering output, not data entry — because at 0.4 mm pitch, the difference is measured in microns and money.

  • Built from the datasheet, not a database

    Every dimension, pin number and package variant is checked against the manufacturer's current drawing and errata.

  • Reviewed by a second engineer

    Pad geometry, pin-1 orientation, courtyard and 3D body are compared before anything reaches your vault.

  • Named the way your team searches

    Part numbers, descriptions and parameters follow your conventions — or we help define a scheme that scales past 10,000 parts.

  • Delivered in your CAD format

PCB design knowledge

The details that decide whether a board works.

A short field guide to the standards and decisions behind every part we build. Use it to brief us, or to sanity-check the library you already have.

A footprint is geometry plus intent.

A land pattern is more than pads. It is pad geometry, solder-mask opening, paste aperture, courtyard and assembly outline — each derived from the package drawing and the density level your process can actually support.

  • Density A (most) leaves the largest pads and 0.5 mm courtyard for hand soldering and rework.
  • Density B (nominal) is the default for mainstream SMT — 0.25 mm courtyard, balanced pads.
  • Density C (least) trades rework access for density: 0.1 mm courtyard, tighter pads.
  • Pin 1 must be unambiguous in copper, silkscreen and the 3D body — not only in the symbol.

Impedance is a stackup decision, not a routing fix.

Once the fab stackup is fixed, trace width and dielectric height set your characteristic impedance. Chasing 50 Ω after routing usually means thinner traces, more layers or a controlled-impedance note — all cheaper to plan early.

  • Keep a continuous reference plane under every high-speed net; splits force return current to detour.
  • A 4-layer 1.6 mm board with 0.2 mm dielectric is a workhorse, but USB and DDR may need tighter spacing.
  • Ask the fab for a stackup with measured Dk/Df and tolerance, not just a layer count.
  • Document target impedance, test coupon and allowed tolerance on the fabrication drawing.

Design for the line that will build it.

DFM is not a final checkbox. Annular ring, mask webs, silkscreen clearance and component spacing all come from the same place: the capability of the fab and the assembly house you chose.

  • Keep at least 0.05 mm annular ring on vias and 0.1 mm mask webs to avoid slivers.
  • Leave 0.15 mm between silkscreen and pads — ink on a pad becomes a solder defect.
  • Add fiducials, tooling holes and a panel rail before the panelization review, not after.
  • Check component spacing for rework access, especially around QFN and BGA corners.

A part is only as good as the data attached to it.

The footprint gets the board built; the data keeps it manufacturable. MPN, manufacturer, lifecycle status and approved alternates decide whether the design survives procurement three months from now.

  • One MPN per library part — no “family” entries that hide package or temperature variants.
  • Record datasheet revision and the date it was checked; datasheets change silently.
  • Track lifecycle state and PCN/EOL notices so obsolescence is a planned change, not a surprise.
  • Carry RoHS/REACH status and approved alternates into the BOM and PLM export.
